# Why Some Companies Move Slower Than Their Competitors — Even With Talented Teams

Dan Jallits · 2026-05-27

Editorial draft based on the complete supplied text.

Executives in large organizations often ask the same question: “Why does it take us so much longer than our competitors to launch something new?”

The initiative might be a new sales channel, an AI capability, an acquisition integration, or a change to fulfillment. The experience is familiar: dates slip, costs rise, teams get frustrated, and the business starts to doubt whether engineering can deliver.

It’s tempting to treat that as an execution problem. Sometimes it is. But before asking talented teams to move faster, I’d look at what they have to work around. Years of fragmented systems and processes can turn an ordinary business change into a coordination project.

## A new initiative inherits the old complexity

An enterprise may have several order management systems, disconnected product information platforms, poorly governed master data, and digital assets spread across repositories. Add applications built for historical exceptions, spreadsheet-based operations, manual reconciliation, and knowledge held by a few people rather than captured in systems.

These problems reinforce one another. Teams encounter conflicting definitions, duplicated logic, unclear ownership, and data they cannot rely on. Every new project has to negotiate the same gaps.

Take a request to launch products in a new marketplace. It sounds like a sales initiative. In practice, it may require rebuilding product data structures, reconciling inventory definitions, normalizing prices, aligning taxonomies, mapping fulfillment processes, and cleaning up digital assets. Then come the custom integrations and temporary manual workflows.

The team isn’t simply launching a channel. It’s doing part of the enterprise’s unfinished modernization work under a launch deadline.

## Parallel work still draws on the same people

A reasonable executive response is to ask whether the organization can repair its foundations while accelerating new initiatives. It can, but the capacity has to be real. Attention, specialist knowledge, and tolerance for change are limited.

Without that capacity, projects tend to absorb the problem in one of two ways. They slow down while teams compensate for weak foundations, or they meet the date by adding more exceptions: another integration, another duplicate data store, another spreadsheet, another manual step.

The second outcome can look like success. It also leaves the next team with more to untangle. Repeated often enough, the effort to deliver quickly makes future delivery harder.

## AI makes the gaps harder to ignore

AI raises expectations for product enrichment, customer support, pricing, forecasting, content generation, and supply-chain optimization. Those capabilities still depend on the operating model underneath them.

If two systems disagree about a product or a customer, an AI workflow needs a reliable way to resolve that disagreement. If nobody owns the data, the model cannot supply ownership. If a process depends on inconsistent manual steps, automating it adds another layer of fragility.

Weak governance introduces operational and compliance risks as well. AI can expose deficiencies that people previously managed through judgment, institutional knowledge, and repeated reconciliation. It does not make those deficiencies disappear.

## Modernization needs a business priority

Leadership has to decide how much capacity goes toward repairing foundations and how much goes toward new initiatives. There is no universal allocation. The mistake is to treat modernization as something engineering will fit around the real work.

Reliable data and consistent processes affect how quickly a business can enter a channel, support a customer, or integrate an acquisition. Platform consolidation and clear governance can reduce coordination costs across many projects. Those are business capabilities worth prioritizing.

That doesn’t mean suspending innovation until the architecture is perfect. It means being explicit about which foundational problems are constraining the outcomes the business wants most.

## Fix the friction that keeps recurring

I’d start with a high-friction operational domain rather than a plan to repair everything. Find the changes that repeatedly stall there, identify the systems and handoffs involved, and establish who owns the capability.

The work may include reducing unnecessary system duplication, standardizing a core process, creating a durable integration pattern, or replacing a spreadsheet-dependent operation. Data governance should precede advanced automation where unreliable data is the bottleneck.

The case for that investment should describe an outcome. “Improve the architecture” is hard to prioritize against a commercial deadline. An explicit target such as reducing a new-channel launch from 12 months to 12 weeks gives leadership something to assess. That is an example of a goal, not a claim that every organization can achieve it.

The aim is to create capabilities that the next initiative can reuse, rather than leave behind another collection of project-specific workarounds.

## Make change less expensive

Accumulated complexity often becomes visible through slow delivery, higher operating costs, exhausted teams, and transformations that never reach their intended outcome. By then, investing in the foundations can feel especially difficult because the business needs results immediately.

Putting the work off does not preserve capacity indefinitely. It passes the same costs to future initiatives, usually with more exceptions attached.

Agile practices can help a team organize its work. Sustainable speed also needs an operating model that allows change without extraordinary effort: clear ownership, dependable systems, governed data, fewer unnecessary handoffs, and priorities that match the available capacity.

When a competitor moves faster, the useful question may be less about how hard its people work and more about how much friction they have to carry.
